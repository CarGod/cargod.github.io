This article has just one purpose: to help us step out of the overwhelming anxiety about AI, gather ourselves, and set off again.

It is a little long. It asks for something scarce in this age: patience.

## 1. The Current of Our Times

As someone exploring AI applications, my job is to study how AI can replace repetitive work.

One of the things I hear most often is: “If you use AI to do it, what are we supposed to do?”

Here is what I want to say: just as taxis displaced rickshaw pullers, even if Benz had not built a car 140 years ago, another company would have.

“No one can stop the march of the times.”

But although rickshaw pullers disappeared, taxi drivers sprang up everywhere.

A rickshaw puller cannot take a passenger from Beijing to Hainan. (Perhaps it is theoretically possible?)

A taxi driver can.

AI is much the same. It brings an exponential increase in productivity and a change in how we work.

Just as rickshaw pullers had to learn to drive, we need to learn about AI: what it does well and what it does not.

Stop dwelling on how AI might replace me. Think about how I can steer AI to improve my productivity.

When we need to dig a tunnel, we use a tunnel boring machine. No one thinks digging with bare hands would be more efficient, and no one develops themselves simply by becoming exceptionally good at digging tunnels.

Likewise, today we should not compete with AI in brainpower. That would be like competing with an excavator in arm strength.

Perhaps we never needed to surpass anything. We need to learn to hand machines the work that belongs to machines,

then look for what makes human beings irreplaceable.

## 2. The Irreplaceable Human

What is AI good at?

I have been thinking about this since 2022. Every time I think I have found the answer, a new technology appears and knocks me flat.

I may not want to admit it, but I have to.

AI is so powerful that sometimes it frightens me, too.

The qualifications I studied so hard for, the experience I take pride in, the skills I rely on to make a living.

In front of it, they seem like a joke...

After being knocked down by AI countless times, something finally dawned on me.

The education I received over the past few decades had been teaching me how to become a better “machine.” I kept learning, accumulating knowledge, and practicing, eventually becoming an expert in a particular field.

I had been learning what earlier generations knew and imitating within established frameworks. And those are precisely the things AI excels at. It is better at being a machine than I am.

It has all the knowledge accumulated since the birth of human civilization, the fastest and most powerful chips for a brain, and it never gets tired.

And me? Just a stretch of the 996 work schedule—9 a.m. to 9 p.m., six days a week—is enough to set off alarms in several of my health indicators.

I am like a stubborn kerosene lamp, giving off its last dim light. Even the faintest breeze could put me out.

AI is like a sun. Its dazzling light is so bright that I cannot open my eyes, let alone catch up with it.

Is it really unbeatable?

It was the autumn of 1666, at Woolsthorpe Manor in Lincolnshire, England. Newton, confined to his home because of the Great Plague of London, was sitting under an apple tree, lost in thought.

Suddenly, a ripe apple fell from the tree and hit Newton on the head, snapping him out of his reverie. Holding his head, Newton angrily picked up the apple. Just as he was about to throw it away, a thought flashed through his mind: why did the apple fall to the ground rather than fly into the sky? Or fly diagonally?

“The Earth must be attracting it! And that attraction must point toward the center of the Earth!”

Newton slapped his thigh and jumped to his feet. Excited, he ran back to his bedroom with the apple and began calculating furiously.

If gravity followed an inverse-square law—twice the distance, one quarter of the force—then the Earth's pull on the Moon should exactly balance the “centrifugal force” produced by the Moon's orbit around the Earth (or, in other words, provide the centripetal force). He compared the acceleration of a falling apple with the acceleration of the Moon in its orbit around the Earth.

But the figure for the Earth's radius available to Newton at the time was inaccurate—the accepted figure was too small—so his results did not match.

You can imagine his disappointment. Believing his theory was wrong, he put the manuscript in a drawer and turned to optics and alchemy.

Newton was only 23 that year.

Eighteen years later, in 1684, Newton had become a widely respected physicist and a star professor at Cambridge University.

The famous astronomer Edmond Halley—the one who discovered Halley's Comet—made a special trip to Cambridge to visit him.

Halley asked a question that was troubling scientists at the time: “If the Sun's gravitational pull on the planets is inversely proportional to the square of the distance, what shape should their orbits be?”

Without hesitation, Newton replied: “An ellipse.”

Shocked, Halley asked: “How do you know?”

Newton answered calmly: “I calculated it before.” (He meant the calculation from 18 years earlier.)

Halley asked him to bring out the manuscript, but Newton could not find it.

So Newton promised to write up the proof again and send it to Halley.

This time, Newton had a more accurate measurement of the Earth's radius from the French astronomer Picard.

At last, he obtained the answer he had been looking for and proved the existence of universal gravitation.

In 1687, at the age of 43, Newton published the greatest work in the history of human science, *Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy*, setting out his three laws of motion.

From being hit by an apple in 1666 to completing the proof and publishing it, Newton took 21 years.

That flash of inspiration, and the later verification, laid the foundations of human physics.

![A person sitting under a tree watches a falling apple and reflects.](assets/inline-02-apple-curiosity-v2.webp)

Imagine returning to 1666, with AI rather than Newton watching the apple fall.

It would not think: “Why did the apple fall to the ground rather than fly into the sky? Or fly diagonally?”

Still less would it return more than 20 years later to correct a result that had been wrong because the original data was wrong.

Today's AI cannot do this, and future AI will not be able to do it either.

That is because it does not truly possess the ability to think as humans do. Whether we call it thinking or deep reasoning, it is fundamentally mathematical computation performed by a computer.

It does not have a true capacity to create. Everything it knows is based on humanity's present understanding of the universe and the world. Everything it creates is a reworking of what already exists.

People are different. When we think, our brain's neurons transmit signals between one quadrillion and ten quadrillion times per second.

That sudden spark of inspiration happens in an instant. AI will never be able to imitate it.

What can be replaced is a person doing a “machine's” work. What cannot be replaced is the human being.

## 3. Creativity, Imagination, and Expression

These three abilities are inscribed deep in the genetic inheritance of every one of us. We are born with them. They are the result of hundreds of millions of years of the universe's remarkable evolution, and no technology can imitate them.

Today, we can ask AI: “Draw a picture of the most unusual alien you can imagine.”

If this article has ten thousand readers and every one of them gives AI that instruction, we will eventually discover that everyone's aliens look more or less alike.

But if we give the same instruction to ten thousand people, some of their aliens may make our eyes light up: creatures we seem never to have imagined or seen before.

A “flash of inspiration” is a mysterious thing. It is neither simple randomness nor deliberate imitation.

For example, today I decide to go out. Halfway out, I suddenly no longer want to, so I head back inside. Halfway back, I want to go out again. Halfway out, I change my mind again. Halfway back, I decide I want to tango my way outside. Halfway through the dance, I decide to switch to ballet. (How eccentric would someone have to be to do all these strange things...)

But that is a human being. A unique human being.

Never underestimate the power of a flash of inspiration. Almost all of humanity's great inventions began with one.

AI can replace designers, but it cannot replace a human designer's inspired adjustment that makes the whole work more appealing.

AI can replace programmers, but it cannot replace a human programmer's inspired commit that dramatically improves an entire system's performance.

AI can replace doctors, but it cannot replace a human doctor's inspired discovery that uncovers the cause of a patient's long-standing illness.

AI can replace people in operations, but it cannot replace an inspired idea from a human operator that multiplies the ROI.

...

Creativity, imagination, and expression are our core competitive strengths. They are also the abilities we most need to develop.

Designers should think of more beautiful work, describe it to AI, and let it generate the result.

Programmers should think of more robust architectures, describe them to AI, and let it generate the result.

Lawyers should think about the clues hidden in case files, tell AI what they are looking for, and let it find them.

![A person sketches an unusual alien on paper, while a laptop displays a finished version of the same idea.](assets/inline-03-imagination-collaboration-v2.webp)

Friends, if you have made it this far, I would like you to say “thank you” to yourself.

In an age when even videos are watched at three times the speed, you possess something as rare as a gemstone: patience.

If there is still something you want to change, do not hesitate. Choose whichever AI feels comfortable to use:

Qianwen, Doubao, Yuanbao, Kimi, Deepseek, Gemini, ChatGPT...

Start using it.

Then keep pushing yourself to think about the essence of the problem. Share your thinking with AI. Reflect on its conclusions, and share your next thoughts with it.

Repeat the process.

Record more of the beautiful things in your life. Look at beautiful things. Practice expressing what is beautiful about them.

AI's capabilities are the same for everyone. The only thing that can create a gap is the person using it.

Every designer can use AI to design images. Some designers immediately see a beautiful work in their mind, describe that picture accurately to AI, and ultimately obtain it.

Other designers have nothing in mind and simply ask AI to do the work. They end up dissatisfied, conclude that AI is rubbish, and stop using it.

Every programmer can use AI to write code. Some start by designing the structure of the whole project and writing the coding standards, then ask AI to implement the architecture step by step.

Others ask AI to get to work immediately: fix one error, introduce another, fix that one, and finally stumble their way to launch, continually piling up “shit-mountain code.”

What looks efficient for a moment brings the pain of long-term maintenance later.

Go, my friends.

Think, discover, create.
